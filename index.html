Research Period

The data for this study was collected over an 8-month period, from November 1, 2025, to July 1, 2026.

Executive Summary

This study examines the profound impact of political conflict, the military coup, economic hardship, educational disruption, natural disasters, and restrictions on freedom of expression on the mental health of people in Myanmar. Based on a survey of 814 respondents across 14 states and regions, the findings reveal a severe mental health crisis.

  • Universal Hopelessness: 100% of respondents report experiencing hopelessness either “Extremely” or “Greatly”.
  • Primary Stressors: The military coup and armed conflict scored the highest average impact (4.4/5), followed closely by economic hardship (4.3/5) and educational barriers (4.1/5).
  • Lack of Support: 85% of respondents stated that they have “Not sufficient at all” or only “Slightly sufficient” access to mental health support services.
  • Gender Disparities: Male respondents generally reported slightly higher average impact scores across several stressors compared to female respondents.

Introduction

Myanmar has faced compounded crises since the 2021 military coup, including widespread armed conflict, economic collapse, and the suppression of civil liberties. These systemic shocks disrupt daily life, destroy livelihoods, and dismantle educational and health infrastructures. The psychological toll of living under constant threat, uncertainty, and adversity is immense. This research aims to quantify the mental health burden on the population and identify the primary drivers of psychological distress.

Research Objectives

  • To determine the distribution of reported mental health symptoms (depression, anxiety, hopelessness, sleep issues) among survey respondents.
  • To explore the statistical association between major socio-political stressors (coup, conflict, economic collapse) and reported mental health outcomes.
  • To compare mental health impacts across different demographic groups (gender, age, occupation) and geographic regions.
  • To assess the perceived availability and accessibility of mental health support services in Myanmar.

Literature Review

According to the World Health Organization (WHO, 2022), mental health is not merely the absence of disease but a state of well-being where an individual can cope with normal stresses and contribute to their community. In conflict-affected settings, the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC, 2007) highlights that populations face elevated risks of depression, anxiety, trauma, and sleep disorders. Economic instability and a lack of educational continuity further compound these vulnerabilities, particularly among youth and working-age adults (OCHA, n.d.).

Research Methodology

Design & Population: A cross-sectional survey design was used, targeting residents within Myanmar. The sample included 814 respondents.

Sampling & Data Collection: Data was collected via a mixed-methods approach, utilizing both online and offline (face-to-face) surveys. Random sampling was attempted, though logistical and security constraints in conflict zones limited strict randomization.

Instrument: The survey captured demographics (age, gender, occupation, region) and measured the impact of various stressors on a 1–5 Likert scale. It also assessed the frequency of symptoms like hopelessness, depression, sleep issues, and panic attacks.

Ethics: All respondent data was anonymized. No personally identifiable information was collected or exposed.

Research Findings

The interactive dashboard above visualizes the core findings. Key takeaways include:

  • The top three demographic groups were 26–35 year olds (53%), Private Employees (42%), and residents of Yangon (16%).
  • Stressors: Conflict and the Coup averaged 4.4/5. Education barriers averaged 4.1/5. Over 96% of respondents reported that the coup “Extremely” or “Greatly” impacted their mental health.
  • Symptoms: 42% experience anxiety frequently, 35% report frequent depressive symptoms, and 29% suffer from frequent sleep difficulties.
  • Support: Only 2% reported “Extremely sufficient” access to mental health support.
